It's generally well known that in startups, you start with generalists to build your company and then move to specialists as you find product-market fit and scale the company. There's something inherently correct in this idea; when there's more work to do than people to do it and very little money coming in to a brand new business, you have to wear a lot of hats to survive. Engineers who can talk to customers to build product understanding, sales reps who can handle operational work or customer support, or designers who can also code are all valuable pairings of skills (among many others) that help startups actually get off the ground.

But once the company starts to grow, businesses move to hire specialists. Again, this feels correct. Once you have the ability to bring on people with serious domain experience, they can help you walk-the-walk faster than figuring it out on your own.

Titans of industry double down on this in their advice to growing startups.

- "The first scale technique to implement is **specialization**. … **In startups, everybody starts out as a jack-of-all-trades.**" -- [Ben Horowitz](https://a16z.com/taking-the-mystery-out-of-scaling-a-company/)
- "as you move from village to city, functions are beginning to be differentiated; you're really multi-threading. ... Specialization at all levels becomes more important." -- [Reid Hoffman](https://assets-global.website-files.com/6446dd70aaa07871c795c474/6537e2547084885cc62e3375_HBR%20-%20Blitzscaling.PDF)

Specialists are undeniably valuable. They help you go deeper in your domain. They can help redefine what's possible and what tasks you can accomplish.

Despite that truth, a company full of specialists will inevitably fail.

As a startup, if you don't focus on retaining your early generalists, you'll lose people who have the greatest degree of specialty in _being productive at your company_. Early hires who are pushed down the hierarchy and replaced with industry veterans often lose the ability to influence the direction of a growing company. Sometimes this is for the best, but in most cases I've seen, companies also then struggle to retain their core identity; what made them work in the first place.

These early generalists know who built the early products. They know _why_ decisions were made as keepers of the company lore. They are deep wells of expertise on the sausage making of how you got to where you are today. That value is irreplaceable if they leave.

They also provide value in another, business critical way: flexibility. Those early generalists have helped get numerous products and features off the ground. They are talented at that early, ambiguous work. If your startup survives long enough to enter the world of multi-product businesses, you'll need people like them again to lay the groundwork of your next big bet.

Even before you can take another bet within your company, macro-conditions may change, competitors may grow or die, or your business might be transformed by technological advances overnight. Generalists handle this uncertainty better than specialists, as shown in the [Good Judgment Project](https://web.stanford.edu/~knutson/nfc/schoemaker16.pdf). Generalists are able to raise the floor of outcomes for your business, preventing catastrophe but also filling gaps that naturally arise over time in team structures.

You'll need specialized talent to build impactful companies. Specialization of labor is probably one of the most impactful innovations of social progress after all! But that's not all that you need.

Don't abandon your generalists. Keep raising the floor; pair your generalists with domain specialists and your company will be able to weather any storm.
